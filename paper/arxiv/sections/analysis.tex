\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

The numbers in this section are from the dev set, with one training run per setting, unless noted.

\subsection{One change at a time}

\Cref{tab:ablation} changes one part of the recipe at a time.
On these single runs, ``yes'' minus ``no'' adds 0.4 dev points and 2.0 external points, and the sigmoid a further 1.3 dev points, most of them on MuSiQue (64\% to 76\%).
Single runs on 100 rows per dataset are noisy: here Nimble falls from 55\% to 43\% with the sigmoid, while on the test set the sigmoid-normalised model is 3.7 points above the softmax over ``yes'' on Nimble.
The words ``correct'' and ``incorrect'' in place of ``yes'' and ``no'' did not help (70.6\% against 71.1\% for the single word, 70.1\% against 71.5\% for the difference).

\subsection{Predicted labels}
\label{sec:labels}

The recipes predict different labels.
On MuSiQue, ``Yes'' is correct for 48\% of the dev rows; the sigmoid-normalised models predict it for 47 to 60\%, and the two softmax recipes for 64\% and 69\%.
On ProofWriter, ``Unknown'' is correct for a third of the rows; the softmax never predicts it and the sigmoid-normalised models predict it for 2 to 12\%, so it stays largely unlearned.

\subsection{Numerical precision}
\label{sec:precision}

The encoder's masked-token logits are around 200, where bf16 steps by 1 or 2 and options would tie or swap, so we compute the masked-token head, the scores and the normalisation in fp32.
Even then, bf16 weights changed MuSiQue accuracy by up to 8 points, presumably through rounding elsewhere in the network; we did not trace where.
We therefore keep fp32 weights under bf16 autocast.
Re-scoring a Qwen checkpoint in another precision moved its dev accuracy by at most 0.8 points.
